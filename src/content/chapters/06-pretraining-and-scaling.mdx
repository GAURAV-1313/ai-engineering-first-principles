You will never pretrain a frontier model. Llama 3 405B took about 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs on up to 16,384 H100 GPUs. So why a chapter on it?

Because many odd things you see in production trace back to pretraining. The model is confidently wrong about this year's figures: its facts were fixed when its data was collected. A 3B model beats much bigger models from two years ago: it saw far more tokens per parameter than older scaling advice suggested. A model scores 90% on a public benchmark and 60% on your eval: it may have seen the benchmark. And "can we fine-tune our documents into it?" has an answer only once you know how knowledge gets in at all.

This chapter is literacy. Leave able to do the compute arithmetic on a napkin, name the data pipeline stages, explain Kaplan versus Chinchilla in two sentences, and turn them into product decisions.

## Objective at scale

Pretraining is the next-token game from [chapter 04](/04-language-modeling-and-sampling/#next-token-prediction), played trillions of times. At every position of every document the model predicts a distribution over the next token, and the loss is the [cross-entropy](/04-language-modeling-and-sampling/#entropy-cross-entropy-perplexity) of the token that actually came next.

```python
for step, batch in enumerate(token_stream):      # batch: [B, T+1] token ids
    inputs, targets = batch[:, :-1], batch[:, 1:]
    logits = model(inputs)                        # [B, T, vocab]
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, targets)         # mean over B*T positions
    loss.backward()
    clip_grad_norm(model, 1.0)                    # see "Training instability"
    optimizer.step(lr=schedule(step)); optimizer.zero_grad()
```

No labels to buy: every token is a training example, which is why the method scales to the whole web. To predict the next token of a court order or a Python file, the model has to absorb facts, grammar and code structure. All of that is a side effect of lowering one number. Two consequences for you:

- **It rewards plausibility, not truth.** A fact seen once is barely learned, but some token must still come next: the root of [hallucination](/04-language-modeling-and-sampling/#hallucination-as-a-training-sampling-property).
- **Knowledge freezes at the data cutoff.** The weights are a lossy picture of the corpus on the day it was collected. The last months before the cutoff are thin, because the web has not finished writing about them.

## Web data

Most of every pretraining corpus comes from **Common Crawl**, a non-profit that publishes regular snapshots of billions of web pages as raw HTML (WARC) and crude extracted text (WET). Serious pipelines re-extract text from the HTML, because menus and cookie banners in WET hurt quality. Labs add higher-value sources: code repositories, Wikipedia, books, arXiv and PubMed papers, maths pages found by classifiers, and multilingual web text.

The scale: FineWeb (Penedo et al. 2024), an open dataset, holds about 15 trillion tokens from 96 Common Crawl snapshots. Llama 3 trained on 15.6T tokens, Qwen2.5 on 18T.

Three properties reach your product:

- **Freshness lags.** The cutoff is the last crawl used, and coverage of the final months is thin.
- **Language skew.** English dominates. Hindi gets a small share of tokens, so knowledge and fluency are thinner, on top of the [token cost](/02-tokenization/#failure-modes-arithmetic-spelling-non-english-cost) of non-Latin scripts.
- **Provenance is murky.** Crawled text carries copyright, personal data and planted content; licences, robots.txt opt-outs and lawsuits shape what labs use and disclose.

## Filtering, dedup, quality classifiers

Raw crawl is mostly junk: spam, SEO pages, templates, machine-generated text. Every lab runs a pipeline roughly like this:

```text
WARC → text extraction → URL blocklists (adult, PII-heavy)
     → language ID (fastText) → heuristic filters
     → dedup: URL → exact hash → MinHash near-dup → repeated lines
     → model-based quality score (keep top X%)
     → decontaminate against benchmarks (n-gram overlap) → tokenize
```

**Heuristic filters** are cheap rules. The Gopher paper (Rae et al. 2021) published a well-known set: keep documents of 50 to 100,000 words with a mean word length of 3 to 10 characters; drop those with too many `#` or ellipsis symbols, over 90% of lines starting with a bullet or over 30% ending in an ellipsis, under 80% of words containing a letter, too few common English stop words, or heavy repeated lines and n-grams. C4 (Raffel et al. 2020) added the familiar "keep only lines ending in terminal punctuation" rule. A small model's [perplexity](/04-language-modeling-and-sampling/#entropy-cross-entropy-perplexity) also flags garbled OCR and keyword spam.

**Deduplication** matters more than it looks. Lee et al. (2021, "Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better") found many near-duplicates in common datasets; removing them cut verbatim memorisation and let models train as well in fewer steps. Exact copies fall to a hash. Near-duplicates (same article, different header) need **MinHash**:

```python
import hashlib

def shingles(text: str, k: int = 5) -> set[str]:
    w = text.lower().split()
    return {" ".join(w[i:i + k]) for i in range(max(1, len(w) - k + 1))}

def minhash(sh: set[str], num_perm: int = 128) -> list[int]:
    # one salted hash per "permutation"; keep the minimum over all shingles
    return [min(int(hashlib.blake2b(f"{seed}:{s}".encode(), digest_size=8).hexdigest(), 16)
                for s in sh) for seed in range(num_perm)]

def est_jaccard(a: list[int], b: list[int]) -> float:
    return sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / len(a)
```

Two documents share a minimum under a random hash with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity, so the fraction of matching slots estimates it. To avoid comparing all pairs, **LSH banding** splits the 128 values into, say, 16 bands of 8; documents equal on any band become candidates. Llama 3 used URL, global MinHash and line-level dedup, the last removing lines seen more than 6 times in each bucket of 30M documents.

**Quality classifiers** go past rules: have a strong LLM label a sample for "educational, well-written?", train a small fast classifier on those labels, score everything. FineWeb-Edu kept about 1.3T tokens of FineWeb's 15T this way and trained better models on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks. The trade-off: a classifier encodes someone's taste, and aggressive filtering drops dialects and minority languages with the spam.

<Callout type="tip">

Your RAG ingestion and fine-tuning sets need the same pipeline at small scale. Duplicated chunks crowd top-k with copies of one passage; duplicated fine-tuning rows teach one example many times.

</Callout>

## Data mix

The **data mix** is the sampling weight of each source during training, and it shapes capability as much as size. Llama 3's final mix was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% maths and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual. Labs pick weights by training small proxy models on candidate mixes, or learn them (DoReMi, Xie et al. 2023). **Annealing** upsamples the best data while the learning rate decays at the end of training; Llama 3 also used short annealing runs to judge whether a small new dataset was worth adding.

**Synthetic data** is now in most mixes: rewritten pages, generated textbooks, model-written solutions checked by a verifier. Phi-1 ("Textbooks Are All You Need", Gunasekar et al. 2023) showed a small code model trained on filtered "textbook-quality" web code plus generated textbooks and exercises punching well above its size. The limits: it inherits the teacher's errors, narrows diversity, and recursive training on model output loses the distribution's tails (Shumailov et al. 2024). It works best where a checker exists, such as code that runs or maths with a known answer. Your version is in [synthetic data](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#synthetic-data).

## Scaling laws (Kaplan, Chinchilla)

**Scaling laws** are the empirical finding that pretraining loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters N, tokens D and compute C, across many orders of magnitude. Fit on cheap small runs, predict a run 1,000× bigger. A common form:

```text
L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β
E = irreducible loss of text; A/N^α = too few params; B/D^β = too few tokens
```

With C ≈ 6·N·D, the question is how to split a fixed C between model size and tokens.

**Kaplan et al. 2020** (OpenAI) said mostly parameters: optimal N grows roughly as C^0.73. Hence GPT-3: 175B parameters on about 300B tokens. **Hoffmann et al. 2022** (DeepMind, "Chinchilla") tuned learning-rate schedules to each run's length and found N and D should grow equally, about 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla, 70B on 1.4T tokens, used the same compute as 280B Gopher (300B tokens), beat it on most tasks and is 4× cheaper to serve. Why the two disagree took two more years to settle: Kaplan counted only non-embedding parameters ([as in chapter 05](/05-transformer-intuition/#parameter-and-flops-accounting)) and fit mostly small models, which bends the exponent upward (Pearce and Song 2024); a fixed warmup too long for small models and untuned optimiser settings did the rest (Porian et al. 2024). Hoffmann's own explanation, the learning-rate schedule, turned out to matter less.

Check against Llama 3's budget:

```text
C = 6·N·D, D = 20·N  →  C = 120·N²  →  N = sqrt(3.8e25 / 120) ≈ 560B on 11T tokens
Meta's own fit on its data:  402B on 16.55T tokens (~41 tokens/param)
Meta shipped:                405B on 15.6T tokens  (~39 tokens/param)
```

"20" belongs to one dataset and setup, not physics. Meta also found the curve flat near the optimum, so modest departures cost little.

### Why modern models train far past Chinchilla

Chinchilla minimises training compute. Serving costs about 2·N FLOPs per token ([FLOPs accounting](/05-transformer-intuition/#parameter-and-flops-accounting)) for the model's whole life, so if you will serve trillions of tokens, a smaller model trained longer is cheaper overall (Sardana et al. 2023, "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal").

| Model | N | D (published) | Tokens per param |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 (2020) | 175B | ~300B | ~1.7 |
| Chinchilla (2022) | 70B | 1.4T | 20 |
| Llama 3 405B (2024) | 405B | 15.6T | ~39 |
| Llama 3.1 8B (2024) | 8.03B | ~15T | ~1,900 |
| Qwen2.5-3B (2024) | 3.09B | 18T | ~5,800 |

That column is why small models are good now; distillation in post-training adds more ([distillation](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#distillation)). The ceiling remains: 3B parameters store fewer rare facts, so a 3B model is fine at extraction over given context and weak on long-tail knowledge.

Scaling laws predict loss, not skills or your task. Apparent sudden "emergent" abilities are partly an artefact of all-or-nothing metrics (Schaeffer et al. 2023). And benchmark scores carry **contamination**: test items leak into crawls, so a high score can be memory. See [benchmarks and contamination](/19-evals-methodology/#benchmarks-and-contamination) and [reading benchmarks critically](/11-serving-and-model-economics/#reading-benchmarks-critically).

## GPU and compute budgets

The formula to carry: **training FLOPs ≈ 6 · N · D**: about 2N per token forward, twice that backward, as in [chapter 05](/05-transformer-intuition/#parameter-and-flops-accounting). Worked for Llama 3 405B, whose N and D are published:

```text
C = 6 × 405e9 × 15.6e12 = 3.79e25 FLOPs          (paper: 3.8 × 10^25)
H100 dense bf16 peak ≈ 989 TFLOP/s; at 40% MFU → ≈ 3.96e14 useful FLOP/s
3.79e25 / 3.96e14 ≈ 9.6e10 GPU-seconds ≈ 26.6M GPU-hours
On 16,384 GPUs ≈ 1,620 hours ≈ 68 days of pure compute
Model card: 30.84M H100-hours (failures, restarts and overhead included)
```

**MFU** (model FLOPs utilisation) is the share of peak hardware FLOPs spent on the model's own maths; Llama 3 reported 38 to 43%. The rest is communication, memory stalls and idle pipeline stages. At an illustrative $2 per H100-hour (rented prices vary several-fold; check current ones), 30.84M GPU-hours is about $62M for the final run, before ablations, data work and people. The formula is the point: cost ≈ GPU-hours × price per GPU-hour.

The same formula at your scale, fine-tuning an 8B model on 10M tokens of your documents:

```text
C = 6 × 8e9 × 1e7 = 4.8e17 FLOPs → / 3.96e14 ≈ 1,200 s ≈ 20 GPU-minutes
```

Cheap compute is not cheap knowledge. Those tokens are 1e7 / 1.5e13 ≈ 7 × 10^-7 of the 15T the model was pretrained on: under one token in a million. Fine-tuning reliably teaches format, style and task behaviour; it does not reliably install facts the model will recall correctly, and it can cause forgetting. So "add our knowledge" usually means retrieval ([prompt vs RAG vs fine-tune](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#prompt-vs-rag-vs-fine-tune-decision)).

## Parallelism intuition

Training needs about 16 bytes per parameter with mixed-precision Adam ([training memory math](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#training-memory-math)): 405e9 × 16 ≈ 6.5 TB, against 80 GB per H100. And 68 days on 16K GPUs is about 3,000 years on one. So the work is split:

```text
Data parallel (DP)      full model per group, different batches, gradients all-reduced
FSDP / ZeRO             DP with weights, grads, optimizer state sharded, gathered just in time
Tensor parallel (TP)    each matmul split across GPUs in a node (talks every layer)
Pipeline parallel (PP)  layers split into stages across nodes; micro-batches flow through
Context parallel (CP)   one long sequence split across GPUs
```

Llama 3 used "4D parallelism" (TP, CP, PP, DP, with FSDP). The rule behind the order: the chattiest split (TP) gets the fastest links inside a node, the least chatty (DP) spans racks. MoE adds expert parallelism ([MoE](/05-transformer-intuition/#mixture-of-experts)). You meet TP again when serving a 70B model across GPUs ([serving parallelism](/10-inference-mechanics/#serving-parallelism)).

## Training instability

**Numerical instability.** The loss jumps (a **loss spike**) and recovers or diverges. Causes include bad batches, attention logits growing without bound, and learning rates too high for the scale. Defences: learning-rate warmup, gradient-norm clipping, normalising queries and keys (QK-norm), an auxiliary z-loss that keeps logits small (PaLM, Chowdhery et al. 2022), and, when a spike will not recover, rolling back to a checkpoint and skipping the offending batches, as the PaLM team did. DeepSeek-V3 (2024) thought it worth reporting that its run had no irrecoverable spikes and no rollbacks.

**Hardware failure.** In one 54-day period of Llama 3 pretraining, Meta logged 466 job interruptions; 419 were unexpected, and about 78% of those were attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware issues. The answer is engineering: frequent checkpoints, fast restarts, automatic detection of bad nodes.

You will see small versions when fine-tuning: a loss that goes to NaN after a learning-rate change, or a spot instance that dies mid-epoch with no checkpoint.

## Base vs chat models

Pretraining produces a **base model**: a document continuer. Ask "Q: What is the capital of France?" and it may continue with more quiz questions. Post-training turns it into a **chat** or **instruct** model ([chapter 07](/07-post-training-and-alignment/#why-post-train)). You prompt a base model by writing the start of the document you want finished:

```text
UPSC Mains 2024, GS Paper III. Model answers by subject experts.

Q4. Discuss the role of MSMEs in employment generation. (150 words)
Answer:
```

When a base model is right:

- **Fine-tuning start point** when you want to own format and behaviour without inheriting a chat template and refusal style.
- **Scoring text.** Base-model log-probs are a cleaner "how typical is this" signal for filtering and anomaly detection; post-training bends a chat model's distribution toward its own style.
- **Diverse generation** for synthetic data seeds, and **pure completion** (autocomplete, code infill).

For instructions, tools, JSON and conversation, use the instruct model. Few hosted APIs expose base models; open-weight families usually publish both (Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct).

## Open-weight vs closed landscape

**Closed** models (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) are API-only: frontier capability and managed serving, but no control of versions, data location or weights, and sizes and data are undisclosed.

**Open-weight** models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, Gemma) publish weights, which is not the same as open source:

- **Licences differ.** Llama uses Meta's community licence with use restrictions. Most Qwen2.5 sizes are Apache 2.0, but Qwen2.5-3B uses the more restrictive "Qwen Research" licence. Check the exact size you deploy.
- **Data is usually secret.** Fully open projects such as AI2's OLMo release data and code too, which you need to check contamination.
- **Disclosure varies.** Llama 3 and DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active per token, 14.8T tokens, 2.788M H800 GPU-hours) published enough for this chapter's arithmetic.

For you the choice is operational: residency, cost at your volume, latency, fine-tunability and acceptable quality. The arithmetic is in [API vs self-hosted](/11-serving-and-model-economics/#api-vs-self-hosted-open-weights) and [local and edge](/11-serving-and-model-economics/#local-and-edge).

**Failure modes.** Facts stop at the cutoff and the model does not know today's date: it "corrects" newer figures back to memorised ones or calls the current fiscal year "future". Fixes, most reliable last: date and cutoff in the prompt, retrieval for anything that changes, a verified fact table that overrides the model. Contamination is the second failure: a leaderboard pick underperforms on your data, and only your held-out eval decides.

**Cost.** Frontier pretraining is tens of millions of dollars per final run, out of scope for app teams. Your levers: the smallest model that passes your eval, fine-tuning for behaviour, retrieval for knowledge. Use 6·N·D to sanity-check any training quote.

**Latency.** Pretraining fixed N, and N sets per-token compute (2·N) and the bytes read per decoded token. An over-trained small model serves faster than a Chinchilla-optimal big one of similar quality.

**Observability.** Log the exact model id with every call and keep each model's stated cutoff. A provider swap changes what the system "knows"; tie spikes in "outdated" complaints to it.

**Security.** Poisoning needs few documents: Souly et al. (2025, Anthropic with the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute) planted a simple backdoor with about 250 documents in models from 600M to 13B parameters ([poisoning and backdoors](/33-security-prompt-injection/#poisoning-and-backdoors)). Models can regurgitate memorised training text, including personal data (Carlini et al. 2021). Record provenance and licence for every source in your own fine-tuning and RAG corpora.

</Production>

<Q q="What do scaling laws tell you?">

- Loss falls as a smooth power law in N, D and C, so labs fit small runs and predict big ones before paying.
- C ≈ 6·N·D; the question is how to split it. Kaplan 2020: mostly parameters (N ∝ C^0.73). Chinchilla 2022: equally, about 20 tokens per parameter; 70B on 1.4T beat 280B Gopher at equal compute.
- The ratio is data-dependent: Meta's Llama 3 fit gave about 40.
- Chinchilla ignores inference, which costs 2·N per token forever, so production over-trains small models: Llama 3.1 8B about 1,900 tokens per parameter, Qwen2.5-3B about 5,800.
- They predict loss, not specific abilities or your task.
- **The trap:** "bigger is always better" or "abilities emerge at size X", missing the inference-cost argument and the metric-artefact evidence.

</Q>

<Q q="Base vs instruct model: when would you use a base model?">

- Base continues documents; instruct is post-trained for instructions, templates, tools and refusals.
- Base as a fine-tuning start when you want to own format and behaviour.
- Base for scoring text (log-probs as a typicality signal), diverse synthetic seeds, and pure completion such as code infill.
- Prompt it as a document prefix, not an instruction.
- Anything user-facing, tool-using or JSON-producing: instruct.
- **The trap:** "base models are uncensored, so better", or not knowing few hosted APIs offer them.

</Q>

<Q q="Why does knowledge cutoff matter for your product design?">

- Weights hold the corpus as of the last crawl, recent months are thin, and the model does not know today's date.
- In fast-moving domains (tax limits, schemes, prices) it confidently "corrects" newer facts to older ones.
- Design: inject today's date and the model's cutoff; retrieve current sources; keep a verified fact table that overrides memory; show "as of" dates.
- Track cutoff per model id; an upgrade changes what the system knows.
- Freshness is a retrieval problem: fine-tuning teaches behaviour more reliably than facts.
- **The trap:** "we'll fine-tune monthly to stay current": costly, unreliable for recall, stale between runs.

</Q>

<Q q="Estimate the training compute and GPU time for a 70B model on 15T tokens.">

- C = 6 × 70e9 × 15e12 = 6.3e24 FLOPs.
- H100 at about 1e15 bf16 peak and 40% MFU: about 4e14 useful FLOP/s.
- 6.3e24 / 4e14 ≈ 1.6e10 GPU-seconds ≈ 4.4M GPU-hours (Meta's card reports 7.0M for Llama 3.1 70B, overhead included).
- On 8,192 GPUs: about 530 hours, roughly 22 days of pure compute.
- Name what it ignores: long-context attention, recomputation, failures, ablations.
- **The trap:** using 2·N·D (forgetting the backward pass) or assuming 100% utilisation.

</Q>

</Interview>

6. Run the same near-dup search between your eval inputs (`tests/retrieval_eval_queries.json`) and any text used as prompt examples or references; print overlaps.
7. Plant 10 near-duplicates (copy a question, change a few words).
