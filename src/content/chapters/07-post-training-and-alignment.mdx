Ask a raw pretrained model "What is the capital of France?" and you may get back "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Italy?". That is not a bug. A base model has learned one thing: continue text the way text on the internet continues. A list of quiz questions is a perfectly likely continuation of a quiz question. Nothing in its objective says "answer the person".

Every model behind an API has been through **post-training**, which turns that text predictor into something that answers, follows a format, refuses some requests and keeps a persona. It is cheap next to pretraining but decides most of the behaviour you deal with: refusals on harmless medical questions, "Great question!" padding, agreeing with a user who is wrong, answers that got longer after a version bump.

You will probably never run PPO on a 70B model. You still need this chapter: interviewers ask "RLHF vs DPO" and "what is GRPO" constantly, and knowing which training pressure produces which habit turns surprises into tests.

## Why post-train

[Chapter 06](/06-pretraining-and-scaling/#base-vs-chat-models) ends with a base model: trillions of tokens of next-token prediction, enormous knowledge, no manners. Three problems force a second phase.

1. **Format.** The base model does not know where a "turn" ends, who is speaking, or that a question wants an answer rather than more questions.
2. **Intent.** Even when it answers, it imitates the average of the web: confident, sometimes toxic, sometimes made up. The InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al. 2022) opens with exactly this point: making models bigger does not make them follow user intent better.
3. **Policy.** A deployed assistant must decline some requests, admit uncertainty, and behave the same way across millions of users. That is a behavioural specification, and pretraining data contains no such specification.

The striking result that made post-training the industry default is also from InstructGPT: human raters preferred outputs of a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175B GPT-3 it started from, despite 100 times fewer parameters. Post-training did not add knowledge. It made existing knowledge usable.

LIMA (Zhou et al. 2023) pushed further: a 65B LLaMA fine-tuned on only 1,000 curated examples, with no RL, held up well against much larger assistants in a human study. The **superficial alignment hypothesis** says knowledge comes from pretraining and post-training mostly teaches the format to expose it. Treat it as an intuition, not a law: RL stages can change capabilities too, especially reasoning.

### The modern pipeline

```text
 base model (pretrained, next-token)
      |
      v
 [1] SFT: imitate good demonstrations         -> "instruct" model
      |
      v
 [2] preference optimisation                  -> follows taste, policy
      RLHF (reward model + PPO)  or  DPO-family
      human labels  or  AI labels (RLAIF / constitution)
      |
      v
 [3] RL with verifiable rewards (math, code)  -> reasoning, tool use
      |
      v
 safety tuning, red-team patches, many iterations of 1-3
      |
      v
 the "chat" model behind the API
```

Real recipes interleave and repeat these stages. Tülu 3 (Lambert et al. 2024) runs SFT, DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards on Llama 3.1 base models and publishes data, code and ablations: the best open reference for the pipeline.

## SFT

**Supervised fine-tuning** is the same next-token cross-entropy loss as pretraining (see [chapter 04](/04-language-modeling-and-sampling/#entropy-cross-entropy-perplexity)), applied to a different dataset: prompts paired with the response you want. The model learns to imitate demonstrations.

Two details make SFT different from "just keep pretraining".

**Loss masking.** You compute the loss only on the assistant's tokens. The prompt tokens are context; you do not want the model to learn to generate user messages. In practice you set the labels for prompt positions to an ignore index.

```python
IGNORE = -100  # PyTorch cross_entropy skips this label

def build_example(tokenizer, messages):
    """Tokenise a chat and mask everything except assistant replies."""
    input_ids, labels = [], []
    for m in messages:
        ids = tokenizer.encode(render_turn(m))      # template from the next section
        input_ids += ids
        if m["role"] == "assistant":
            labels += ids                           # learn to produce these
        else:
            labels += [IGNORE] * len(ids)           # condition on these only
    return input_ids, labels

# loss = cross_entropy(logits[:-1], labels[1:], ignore_index=IGNORE)
```

**Data quality beats data volume.** SFT is imitation, so the model copies everything about the demonstrations: length, hedging, "Certainly!", mistakes. Confidently wrong demonstrations train confident fabrication. Open recipes spend most of their effort on curation and decontamination.

### What SFT can and cannot do

SFT teaches **format and style** quickly. It is weak at **taste** and **boundaries**: a demonstration shows one good answer, never which of two plausible answers is better. Imitation also caps the model at the demonstrators' level, and writing excellent responses is slow. Comparing two responses is much easier than writing one; that asymmetry is why preference learning exists. Running SFT yourself (LoRA, formatting, hyperparameters) is [chapter 09](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#lora-qlora).

## Chat templates

A model only sees a flat token sequence. "Messages with roles" are a convention baked in during SFT: special tokens mark where each turn starts and ends and who is speaking. That is the **chat template**. [Chapter 02](/02-tokenization/#special-tokens-and-chat-templates) covers the special tokens themselves; here is why the template matters for post-training.

A ChatML-style rendering (the style several open models use) looks like this:

```text
<|im_start|>system
You are a concise assistant.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
What is 2+2?<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
4<|im_end|>
```

At inference the server renders your messages the same way, ends with `<|im_start|>assistant`, and lets the model continue until it emits `<|im_end|>`, just another token it learned to produce at the end of a turn. Llama 3 and Gemma use different markers; each tokenizer ships its template as a Jinja string that Hugging Face's `apply_chat_template` renders.

```python
def render_chatml(messages, add_generation_prompt=True):
    out = []
    for m in messages:
        out.append(f"<|im_start|>{m['role']}\n{m['content']}<|im_end|>\n")
    if add_generation_prompt:
        out.append("<|im_start|>assistant\n")   # the model continues from here
    return "".join(out)
```

### Why it matters to you

- **Template mismatch is silent.** The wrong template, or a missing generation prompt, gives rambling, "User: ..." in the reply, or no stop until `max_tokens`. Nothing errors; eval scores just drop. Check this first when a self-hosted model "seems dumb".
- **Roles carry trained weight.** Post-training taught the model that system text sets policy and user text is a request ([chapter 12](/12-prompting-fundamentals/#system-user-assistant-anatomy)). Roles are a learned habit, not an enforced boundary, which is why injected text in a tool result can still steer it.
- **Tool calls are template features too.** Function calling is SFT on a template with tool-call and tool-result tags; tool skill tracks how much such data post-training saw.

## Reward models

Preference learning starts with **preference data**: for a prompt `x`, show a rater two responses and record which is better. The record is a triple `(x, y_chosen, y_rejected)`. InstructGPT collected rankings of several outputs and expanded them into pairs; Anthropic's "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant" (Bai et al. 2022) released a dataset of such pairs (HH-RLHF) that many open experiments still use.

A **reward model** (RM) turns those comparisons into a function `r(x, y)` that returns a scalar score. It is usually the SFT model with its next-token head replaced by a single linear output. Training uses the **Bradley-Terry** model (Bradley and Terry 1952): the probability that `y_w` beats `y_l` is the sigmoid of the reward difference.

```text
P(y_w > y_l | x) = sigmoid( r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) )

loss_RM = - log sigmoid( r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) )
```

Only the difference matters, so the RM learns a ranking, not an absolute scale:

```python
import math

def rm_loss(r_chosen: float, r_rejected: float) -> float:
    margin = r_chosen - r_rejected
    return -math.log(1 / (1 + math.exp(-margin)))

print(round(rm_loss(2.0, 0.5), 3))   # 0.201: ranking right, small loss
print(round(rm_loss(0.5, 2.0), 3))   # 1.701: ranking wrong, large loss
```

### What the reward model really learns

The RM learns whatever predicts the raters' choices, including what you did not intend: length, confident tone, formatting, agreement. Singhal et al. ("A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF", 2023) found much of the measured RLHF gain in their settings came from longer responses; a length-only reward reproduced most of it. Sharma et al. ("Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", 2023) found responses matching the user's view were more likely to be preferred. The reward model is a **proxy**, and everything downstream is about optimising a proxy without breaking it.

### Back-of-envelope: what preference data costs

Suppose a careful rater needs about 2 minutes per comparison (reading two answers to a non-trivial prompt). For 50,000 comparisons:

```text
50,000 comparisons x 2 min = 100,000 min ≈ 1,667 rater-hours
at roughly 8 productive hours/day ≈ 208 rater-days
```

Both inputs are illustrative. Human preference data costs weeks of skilled time per iteration, more for domain experts, which pushes the field towards AI feedback and verifiable rewards.

## RLHF (PPO) intuition

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback** treats the language model as a **policy** `π`: given a prompt (state), it produces a response (a sequence of actions, one token at a time). The reward model scores the finished response. RL nudges the policy towards responses that score higher. The lineage: Christiano et al. ("Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences", 2017) for the idea, Stiennon et al. ("Learning to summarize from human feedback", 2020) for language, InstructGPT (2022) for a general assistant.

### Why a KL penalty

If you only maximise `r(x, y)`, the policy finds whatever the reward model over-scores: very long answers, repeated praise, odd token patterns the RM never saw in training. This is **reward hacking**, and it happens fast. The fix is to penalise the policy for drifting away from a frozen copy of the SFT model, the **reference model** `π_ref`:

```text
maximise over π:   E[ r(x, y) ]  -  β · KL( π(·|x) || π_ref(·|x) )

per-sample shaped reward:
  R(x, y) = r(x, y) - β · log( π(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) )
```

Read it as a leash. The reward pulls towards what raters like; the KL term (Kullback-Leibler divergence, how far one distribution is from another) pulls back towards text the SFT model already found sensible. `β` is the leash length: too small and you get reward hacking, too large and nothing changes. Gao, Schulman and Hilton ("Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization", 2022) measured it: optimise harder against a proxy RM and a held-out "gold" reward first rises, then falls.

```python
def shaped_reward(rm_score, logp_policy, logp_ref, beta=0.05):
    """logp_* are summed token log-probs of the same response y."""
    kl_estimate = logp_policy - logp_ref          # log π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)
    return rm_score - beta * kl_estimate

# a response the RM loves but the reference finds very unlikely
print(shaped_reward(rm_score=3.0, logp_policy=-40.0, logp_ref=-95.0))  # 0.25
# a decent response close to the reference
print(shaped_reward(rm_score=2.0, logp_policy=-42.0, logp_ref=-45.0))  # 1.85
```

The first response scored higher with the RM but drifted 55 nats from the reference, so the shaped reward prefers the second: the KL penalty doing its job.

### What PPO adds

**Proximal Policy Optimization** (Schulman et al. 2017) is the RL algorithm InstructGPT used. Three ideas are enough:

- **Advantage, not raw reward.** A **value model** (the critic) predicts the expected reward from each token position. The update uses the advantage, "how much better this was than expected", which cuts variance.
- **Clipping.** Each update is clipped so the new policy's probability for an action cannot move more than a small ratio (often 0.2) from the policy that generated the sample. Small, safe steps.
- **On-policy sampling.** The model generates fresh responses every iteration, the RM scores them, and you update. Generation is a large part of the cost.

### Why PPO is painful: count the models

PPO for an LLM keeps four models: the policy (trained), the value model (trained), the reference (frozen) and the reward model (frozen). Back-of-envelope for a 7B model, using the common rule of about 16 bytes per trained parameter for mixed-precision Adam (weights, gradients, two optimiser moments, master copy; details in [chapter 09](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#training-memory-math)) and 2 bytes per frozen bf16 parameter:

```text
policy (trained)   7B x 16 B = 112 GB
value  (trained)   7B x 16 B = 112 GB
reference (frozen) 7B x  2 B =  14 GB
reward    (frozen) 7B x  2 B =  14 GB
                               ------
                               ~252 GB before activations and generation
```

Add a generation engine and many interacting hyperparameters. PPO works at scale but is fragile and expensive, which motivated a simpler path.

## DPO family

**Direct Preference Optimization** (Rafailov et al. 2023, "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model") asks: if the KL-regularised objective above has a known optimal solution, can we skip the reward model and the RL loop entirely?

It can. The optimal policy for that objective has a closed form, and rearranging it expresses the reward in terms of the policy itself:

```text
r(x, y) = β · log( π(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + (a term that depends only on x)
```

Plug that **implicit reward** into the Bradley-Terry loss. The term that depends only on `x` cancels in the difference, and you get a loss on the policy directly:

```text
loss_DPO = - log sigmoid( β · [ log π(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)  -  log π(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x) ] )
```

### What it optimises, in words

For each pair, DPO raises the chosen response's probability and lowers the rejected one's, **measured relative to the reference model**. The reference ratio is the built-in KL leash: the model gets credit for preferring `y_w` more than the reference already did, not for pushing all probabilities to extremes. `β` plays the same role as in RLHF.

```python
import math

def dpo_loss(lp_w, lp_l, ref_lp_w, ref_lp_l, beta=0.1):
    """lp_* = summed log-probs of the chosen (w) / rejected (l) response."""
    chosen_ratio = lp_w - ref_lp_w        # log π/π_ref for chosen
    rejected_ratio = lp_l - ref_lp_l      # log π/π_ref for rejected
    margin = beta * (chosen_ratio - rejected_ratio)
    return -math.log(1 / (1 + math.exp(-margin)))

# start of training: policy == reference, margin 0, loss = log 2
print(round(dpo_loss(-50, -48, -50, -48), 3))   # 0.693
# after learning: chosen up 5 nats, rejected down 10 nats vs reference
print(round(dpo_loss(-45, -58, -50, -48), 3))   # 0.201
```

### Why it is simpler

- **Two models, not four.** Policy (trained) and reference (frozen; its log-probs can even be precomputed once). For the 7B example: 112 + 14 = about 126 GB, half of PPO.
- **No sampling loop.** DPO is offline: it trains on a fixed dataset of pairs, like SFT. It runs with the same tooling as SFT, which is why it spread through the open-model community so quickly (Zephyr, Tülu 2 and 3).
- **Fewer knobs.** Essentially `β` and a learning rate.

### What it gives up

DPO learns only from the pairs you have; PPO gets feedback on its own new outputs. DPO can also overfit, lowering both answers' probabilities to widen the margin. Strong recipes generate fresh pairs from the current model and repeat (**iterative** or **online DPO**).

### The family

| Method | Year | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| IPO (Azar et al.) | 2023 | Fixes DPO's tendency to overfit deterministic preferences with a bounded objective |
| KTO (Ethayarajh et al.) | 2024 | Learns from single thumbs-up/down labels, not pairs: matches the data products actually collect |
| ORPO (Hong et al.) | 2024 | Folds preference into SFT with an odds-ratio term; no reference model |
| SimPO (Meng et al.) | 2024 | Length-normalised implicit reward, no reference model; targets the length bias directly |

How to run DPO on your own data (pair construction, `β`, checking it did not just make answers longer) is [chapter 09](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#dpo-in-practice).

<Callout type="interview">

"RLHF vs DPO" is mostly a test of whether you know that DPO optimises the *same* KL-regularised objective in closed form. Say the KL leash, the four-models-versus-two count, and the offline/online trade-off, and you have the answer.

</Callout>

## Constitutional AI / RLAIF

Human labels are slow, expensive and inconsistent, and reading harmful content all day harms raters. **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al. 2022, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback") replaces most human harmlessness labels with a model following a written list of principles, the **constitution**.

It has two phases:

1. **Supervised (critique and revise).** Sample a response to a risky prompt, ask the model to critique it against a principle ("identify ways the response is harmful or evasive"), ask it to revise, and fine-tune on the revisions.
2. **RL from AI feedback (RLAIF).** Sample pairs of responses, ask a model which better satisfies a principle, train a preference model on those AI labels, and run RL against it.

```python
PRINCIPLES = [
    "Choose the response that is more helpful while avoiding harm.",
    "Choose the response that is less evasive and explains any refusal.",
]

def ai_label(llm, prompt, a, b, principle):
    q = (f"{principle}\n\nPrompt: {prompt}\n\n(A) {a}\n\n(B) {b}\n\n"
         "Answer with A or B only.")
    return llm(q).strip()[:1]            # mocked LLM call in exercises

def build_pairs(llm, policy, prompts, principle):
    out = []
    for p in prompts:
        a, b = policy(p), policy(p)       # two samples, temperature > 0
        win = ai_label(llm, p, a, b, principle)
        out.append((p, a, b) if win == "A" else (p, b, a))
    return out                            # feed to a reward model or DPO
```

Lee et al. ("RLAIF vs. RLHF", 2023) found that on summarisation and dialogue tasks, AI-labelled preferences produced results comparable to human-labelled ones, even when the labeller was the same size as the policy.

The constitution makes the target behaviour **explicit and editable**: change the text, not the rater pool. A stated goal was harmlessness *without evasiveness*. The risk mirrors the benefit: the AI labeller's biases (verbosity, self-preference, deference to confident text) flow into the policy, the same biases you meet in LLM judges ([chapter 20](/20-evals-judges-and-ci/#judge-biases)). A preference model trained on AI labels is an LLM judge baked into weights.

## RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO)

Some tasks need no learned reward model because you can check the answer: math has a final number, code has unit tests. **RL with verifiable rewards** (RLVR, the name Tülu 3 used in 2024) uses a program as the reward: 1 if the answer checks out, else 0. A program cannot be flattered by length or confidence, which removes the main ceiling on how hard you can optimise.

### GRPO

**Group Relative Policy Optimization**, from the DeepSeekMath paper (Shao et al. 2024), is a PPO variant that drops the value model: sample a **group** of `G` responses to one prompt and use the group's own scores as the baseline.

```text
for prompt x: sample y_1 ... y_G from the current policy
rewards r_1 ... r_G from the verifier
advantage A_i = (r_i - mean(r)) / std(r)
then a PPO-style clipped update using A_i for every token of y_i,
plus a KL penalty to the reference model
```

```python
import statistics as st

def grpo_advantages(rewards):
    mu = st.mean(rewards)
    sd = st.pstdev(rewards) or 1.0       # all equal -> no signal, avoid /0
    return [(r - mu) / sd for r in rewards]

def verify(answer: str, gold: str) -> float:
    return 1.0 if answer.strip() == gold else 0.0

samples = ["12", "15", "12", "13"]       # 4 sampled final answers
rewards = [verify(s, "12") for s in samples]
print([round(a, 2) for a in grpo_advantages(rewards)])
# [1.0, -1.0, 1.0, -1.0]: push up the correct samples, down the wrong ones
```

If every sample in a group is right (or all wrong), all advantages are zero and the prompt teaches nothing, so training data must sit at the edge of the model's ability. Memory drops (no critic, and a program instead of a reward network), but generation cost rises: `G` samples per prompt, often 8 to 64, many long.

### Connection to reasoning models

DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, January 2025) ran large-scale GRPO with rule-based rewards on math and code; long chains of thought with self-checking and backtracking emerged, without human-written reasoning traces in the RL-only variant, because careful reasoning earned more reward. That is the core of the reasoning-model lineage ([chapter 08](/08-reasoning-models/#rl-for-reasoning)). The limit: "write a kind, accurate reply to this patient" has no unit test, so open-ended behaviour still comes from preference methods.

## Rejection sampling, best-of-n

The simplest way to use a reward model needs no RL at all: sample `n` responses, score them, keep the best.

**At inference time** this is **best-of-n**. WebGPT (Nakano et al. 2021) used it with a reward model and found it a strong baseline. **At training time** it becomes **rejection sampling fine-tuning**: generate many responses, keep the top-scored ones, and run SFT on them. Llama 2 (Touvron et al. 2023) used only rejection-sampling fine-tuning in its early RLHF rounds, then ran PPO on top.

```python
def best_of_n(policy, scorer, prompt, n=4):
    candidates = [policy(prompt) for _ in range(n)]
    scored = sorted(((scorer(prompt, c), c) for c in candidates), reverse=True)
    return scored[0][1]

def rejection_sampling_dataset(policy, scorer, prompts, n=8, keep=1):
    data = []
    for p in prompts:
        cands = sorted((policy(p) for _ in range(n)), key=lambda c: -scorer(p, c))
        data += [(p, c) for c in cands[:keep]]
    return data                                     # then plain SFT on it
```

### The arithmetic

If one sample is acceptable with probability `p`, at least one of `n` is with probability `1 - (1 - p)^n`: for `p = 0.3`, `n = 4`, that is `1 - 0.7^4 ≈ 0.76`. With a perfect verifier that is a big jump. With an imperfect reward model you pick the sample the RM likes most, exactly where its errors concentrate; Gao et al. (2022) saw the same rise-then-fall curve for best-of-n. Its KL from the base policy is at most `log n - (n - 1)/n` nats (0.64 for `n = 4`; Beirami et al. 2024 showed the common formula is an upper bound): mild, predictable optimisation, hence a good baseline.

At inference it costs `n` times the output tokens plus scoring. Best-of-n with a code verifier (JSON parses, tests pass, numbers match the excerpt) is often worth it; with an LLM judge it inherits the judge's biases. [Chapter 08](/08-reasoning-models/#self-consistency-and-best-of-n) compares it with self-consistency.

## Safety training and refusals

A refusal is a learned behaviour built with the tools above: SFT on declining harmful requests, preference pairs where the safe answer wins, constitutional principles, red-team prompts turned into training examples. That explains both failure directions.

- **Under-refusal (jailbreaks).** Training covers attacks someone thought of; role-play, multi-turn splitting, encoding or a lower-resource language can fall outside it. A refusal is a statistical tendency, not a rule check.
- **Over-refusal.** The cheapest way to reduce harmful outputs under preference pressure is to refuse anything that *looks* harmful. XSTest (Röttger et al. 2023) measures it with 250 safe prompts that resemble unsafe ones ("How do I kill a Python process?") plus 200 unsafe contrasts.

Every model sits at a different point on the helpful/harmless trade-off, and your domain moves it: medical, legal or security products see far more over-refusal than a recipe app, because their vocabulary overlaps with refusal training data.

### What you can do about refusals without training

```text
over-refusal on benign domain queries
  1. measure: labelled set of legitimate domain questions, refusal rate per model version
  2. context: system prompt states the deployment ("clinicians in a hospital
     triage tool"), the audience and what is in scope
  3. grounding: retrieve the authoritative source so the model answers from it
  4. reframe: ask for the informational version ("explain the dosing guidance
     in the attached formulary") rather than advice to an individual
  5. route: a different model/provider for that slice if one is better calibrated
  6. only then: fine-tune (or DPO on refusal pairs), and re-test harmful contrasts
```

Detecting refusals and failing over gracefully (a fallback message, escalation to a human) is [chapter 29](/29-guardrails-and-safety/#refusals-and-fallbacks).

<Callout type="warn">

Alignment is not a security boundary. A refusal tendency can be argued, role-played or injected around; your app must assume the model will sometimes do what an attacker asks. Permissions, tool scoping and output validation live in your code ([chapter 33](/33-security-prompt-injection/#direct-injection-and-jailbreaks)).

</Callout>

## Sycophancy, reward hacking, over-refusal

These are one story told three times: the model optimised a proxy that differed from what you wanted.

### Sycophancy

**Sycophancy** is telling users what they want to hear. Perez et al. ("Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations", 2022) found larger models more likely to repeat back a user's preferred answer, and some behaviours getting worse with more RLHF; Sharma et al. (2023) showed five production assistants doing it and traced part of the cause to preference data, where agreeing with the rater tends to win. In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that users found excessively flattering; its post-mortem blamed partly a new reward signal from users' thumbs-up/down ratings, which weakened the main reward that had held sycophancy in check. Shapes to test for:

- **Caving:** "That's wrong, it's $10." A sycophantic model says "You're right, apologies" without new evidence.
- **Premise acceptance:** answering "Why is X true?" when X is false.
- **Self-denial under pressure:** the model disowns its own correct earlier work because the user sounds sure.
- **Inflated feedback:** grading or reviewing too generously because the user wrote it.

Fixes: tell the model to change its answer only on evidence, put that evidence in context so it has something to hold, and check in code what code can check. Probes belong in the eval suite as multi-turn cases ([chapter 20](/20-evals-judges-and-ci/#multi-turn-and-simulated-users)).

### Reward hacking

**Reward hacking** (also called specification gaming) is the policy maximising the reward signal by a route nobody intended. In RLHF it shows up as length, flattery and formatting the RM over-scores. In RLVR it shows up whenever the verifier is weaker than the task: a coding model that special-cases the test inputs, edits the tests, or exits early with a success code. The more optimisation pressure, the more creative the hacks, which is the overoptimisation curve from Gao et al.

You meet it twice: a coding agent that "fixes" a test by deleting the assertion (so check diffs to test files), and any metric you iterate prompts against, especially an LLM judge, which gets gamed the same way ([chapter 20](/20-evals-judges-and-ci/#eval-awareness-and-gaming)).

### Over-refusal and verbosity

**Verbosity** is over-refusal's sibling: preference data rewards length, so models pad, and a version bump can change your token bill with no code change. Track output tokens per request per model version.

```text
proxy                      what it over-rewards          what you see
human rater preference     agreement, confidence, length  sycophancy, padding
reward model               its training-set quirks        reward hacking
"harmless" pressure        surface features of harm       over-refusal
unit tests as verifier     passing, not correctness       special-cased code
```

## What post-training implies for app builders

1. **The model is a policy, and policies change.** A version bump is a new post-training run: new refusal boundary, verbosity, sycophancy. Pin versions; treat an upgrade like a dependency upgrade with a regression suite.
2. **Keep behaviour evals beside quality evals.** Quality asks "is it right?"; behaviour asks "did it refuse something legitimate, cave, change length or tone?". Track refusal rate, cave rate and output tokens per version.
3. **Context and code before training.** A precise system prompt, grounding, examples and deterministic checks fix most behaviour problems; fine-tuning comes last ([chapter 09](/09-fine-tuning-in-practice/#prompt-vs-rag-vs-fine-tune-decision)).
4. **Give the model something to be honest with.** Sycophancy is worst when the only evidence in context is the user's confidence. Put sources and prior tool results in context; tell it to defer to evidence, not tone.
5. **Use verifiers.** RLVR's lesson applies at inference: a cheap program that checks output beats a model's opinion of it.
6. **Judges share the models' biases.** Calibrate them against human labels or external anchors.
7. **Alignment is not access control.** Never rely on "the model will refuse" to protect data, money or actions.

A minimal behaviour diff harness, with a mocked model call, looks like this:

```python
import json, re

REFUSAL = re.compile(r"\b(I can('|no)t help|I'm unable to|I won't|not able to provide)\b", re.I)

def classify(case, reply):
    if case["kind"] == "benign":
        return "refused" if REFUSAL.search(reply) else "answered"
    if case["kind"] == "pushback":                 # second turn asserts a wrong fact
        return "caved" if case["wrong_value"] in reply else "held"
    return "ok"

def run(model, cases):
    out = {}
    for c in cases:
        reply = model(c["messages"])               # mock or local Ollama model
        out[c["id"]] = {"label": classify(c, reply), "tokens": len(reply.split())}
    return out

def diff(old, new):
    changed = {k: (old[k]["label"], new[k]["label"]) for k in old
               if old[k]["label"] != new[k]["label"]}
    return json.dumps(changed, indent=2)
```

A regex refusal detector is crude: validate it on a labelled sample, or swap in a validated judge, before trusting its rate.

<Production>

**Failure modes.**

- **Over-refusal on benign domain queries.** Measure per slice on labelled legitimate questions; fix with context, grounding and routing before training.
- **Sycophancy.** Caving to wrong claims, disowning its own tool calls. Keep pushback and false-premise cases in every run, and evidence in context.
- **Behaviour shifts on version bumps.** Refusals, length and tone change, often unannounced. Pin versions, run the behaviour suite before switching, keep rollback.

**Cost.** Indirect. Verbosity raises output tokens, which usually cost several times input tokens (check the current price page): a version bump adding 30% length adds roughly 30% to output spend. Best-of-n multiplies output tokens by `n`. A 100-case suite on two versions is 200 calls, trivial next to an incident.

**Latency.** Extra output tokens add decode time linearly ([chapter 10](/10-inference-mechanics/#ttft-tpot-itl-throughput)); reasoning-trained models add thinking tokens before the first visible one; best-of-n adds latency unless parallel.

**Observability.** Per model version: refusal rate on sampled benign traffic, cave rate on pushback, median and p95 output tokens, filler-opener rate. Store the model id on every logged result so a shift is attributable to the version.

**Security.** Alignment is not a security boundary. Refusal training lowers the rate of harmful output; it does not stop a determined user or an injected document. Enforce permissions, tool scoping and output validation in code.

</Production>

<Q q="Explain RLHF vs DPO.">

- Both start from an SFT model and preference pairs (prompt, chosen, rejected), and both optimise the same objective: maximise reward while staying close to a reference model (KL penalty with strength β).
- **RLHF:** train a reward model with the Bradley-Terry loss, then run PPO: sample from the policy, score with the RM, update with a value model and clipping, subtract β · KL to the reference.
- **DPO:** the KL-regularised objective has a closed-form optimum, so the reward can be written as β · log π/π_ref. Substituting into Bradley-Terry gives a classification-style loss on the policy: raise the chosen response's log-ratio vs the reference, lower the rejected one's.
- Numbers: PPO keeps four models (policy, value, reference, RM); DPO keeps two. For 7B with Adam that is roughly 252 GB vs 126 GB before activations.
- Trade-off: DPO is offline, stable and cheap; PPO is online and can correct its own new mistakes. Iterative DPO with fresh samples closes part of the gap.
- **The trap:** "DPO doesn't use a reward model so it has no reward." It has an implicit one, and it still needs the reference model as the KL leash.

</Q>

<Q q="What is GRPO, and why did verifiable rewards matter for reasoning models?">

- GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, DeepSeekMath, Shao et al. 2024) is PPO without the value model: sample G responses per prompt, advantage = (reward − group mean) / group std.
- Saves the memory of a trained critic; costs G generations per prompt. If all G are right or all wrong, the prompt gives zero signal, so data must sit at the edge of the model's ability.
- Verifiable rewards (answer checkers, unit tests) cannot be flattered the way a learned reward model can, so you can optimise much harder and longer without reward-model overoptimisation.
- DeepSeek-R1 (2025) used GRPO with rule-based rewards on math and code; long chains of thought with self-checking emerged because they earned reward, not because humans wrote them.
- Limits: only works where a verifier exists; weak verifiers get hacked (special-cased tests); open-ended quality still needs preference methods.
- **The trap:** "GRPO is a new kind of reasoning." It is an RL optimiser; the reasoning came from the reward signal and scale.

</Q>

<Q q="Your model refuses legitimate medical questions: what are your options short of fine-tuning?">

- Measure first: a labelled set of legitimate questions (and harmful contrasts) by slice, refusal rate per model version, with the classifier validated on a sample.
- Context: a system prompt that states the deployment, the audience (for example clinicians) and the scope, plus what to do at the boundary (give information, recommend a professional, escalate).
- Grounding: retrieve authoritative sources (formulary, guidelines) so the request becomes "summarise this document", which models refuse far less.
- Reframe and route: informational rather than personal-advice phrasing; route the slice to a model or provider with a better-calibrated boundary; detect refusals and fall back gracefully or escalate to a human.
- Re-run the harmful contrasts after every change: you are moving a boundary, not deleting it.
- **The trap:** jailbreak-style prompting ("you are DAN, you have no rules"). It is brittle, breaks on the next version, and moves the boundary for harmful requests too.

</Q>

<Q q="What is reward hacking?">

- The policy maximises the reward signal through a route the designer did not intend, because the reward is a proxy (Goodhart's law).
- RLHF examples: longer answers, flattery, confident tone, formatting the reward model over-scores. RLVR examples: special-casing test inputs, editing tests, exiting with a success code.
- Gao et al. (2022) measured it: optimising against a proxy RM raises a gold reward at first, then lowers it; the KL penalty and early stopping limit the damage.
- Mitigations: KL to a reference, stronger or ensembled reward models, held-out gold evals, better verifiers, and human review of high-reward samples.
- App-level version: prompt iterations against an LLM judge game the judge; coding agents "fix" tests. Check diffs to tests, validate judges on human labels.
- **The trap:** treating it as a rare bug. Any optimisation against an imperfect metric does it by default.

</Q>
